Why Cash Flows Break Simple Return

The last chapter ended with a warning: simple return only works because nothing happens to the money in between. This chapter makes good on that warning — not with theory, but with one worked example where a single contribution turns a defensible-looking return into a genuinely misleading one.

Take a portfolio worth $100,000 on January 31. By February 15 it's worth $108,000 — except $10,000 of that is a fresh contribution, not market performance. By February 28 it closes the period at $119,400, with nothing else moving in or out. Three data points, one cash flow sitting right in the middle.

Ask simpleReturn() the obvious question over the full period — (End Value − Start Value) ÷ Start Value — and it answers 19.40%: ($119,400 − $100,000) ÷ $100,000. That's the number a "no cash flows" formula hands you without complaint. But $10,000 of that $19,400 increase walked in the door as a deposit on February 15, not as a gain the portfolio earned. Subtract it back out and the portfolio's own dollars moved by $9,400 over the period, not $19,400.

It gets stranger up close. Look only at the February 15 row: value rose from $100,000 to $108,000, an $8,000 increase that simpleReturn() reports as cheerful +8.00%. Except $10,000 of that $108,000 is the fresh deposit — which means the $100,000 that was already in the account actually fell to $98,000 before the new money ever arrived. That sub-period lost $2,000 of the original capital and still posted a positive return, because the deposit was large enough to paper over the loss.

simpleReturn() isn't malfunctioning in either case. It's doing the one job it knows to do — comparing two numbers with no memory of what happened between them. The mistake is asking it a question about a period that wasn't actually clean. See both distortions computed live, from the same numbers, on the Interactive tab.

Actually fixing this — producing one number that correctly accounts for both the size of a cash flow and exactly when it arrived — takes Modified Dietz or Time-Weighted Return, both waiting in the Intermediate tier. Beginner tier has two more pieces to add first, though: how returns compound across clean, cash-flow-free periods (the next chapter, Compounding and Geometric Linking) and how to annualize one. Neither of those problems goes away just because this one exists.

simpleReturn(100000, 119400) only sees two numbers and a $19,400 gap between them — it has no way to know that $10,000 of that gap walked in the door as a deposit. Subtract the cash flow back out (End − Start − Cash Flow) and the portfolio's own dollars moved by , not $19,400. Turning that dollar figure into a correct percentage — one that fairly accounts for when the $10,000 arrived — is exactly what the Intermediate tier is for.

  • Simple return assumes nothing enters or leaves the portfolio between Start Value and End Value — a cash flow anywhere in between breaks that assumption.
  • A period's naive simpleReturn() folds any cash flow's dollar amount into the reported gain — enough of a contribution can even make a sub-period poss a positive return while the original capital actually lost money.
  • The true dollar movement of a period containing a cash flow is End Value − Start Value − Cash Flow, not the raw difference simple return uses.
  • SimpleReturn() itself isn't wrong — it still works correctly on any cash-flow-free sub-period. The mistake is applying it across a period that isn't actually clean.
  • Turning a cash-flow-adjusted dollar figure into a correct percentage return needs Modified Dietz or Time-Weighted Return, both covered in the Intermediate tier.
