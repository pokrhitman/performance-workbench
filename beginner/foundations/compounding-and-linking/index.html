Compounding and Geometric Linking

A portfolio gains 10% one quarter and loses 10% the next. What's the return over both quarters combined? The intuitive answer is 0% — and the gain and loss cancel out. The actual answer is −1.00%. Not close to zero, and not a rounding quirk: returns don't add, they compound, and the difference between those two ideas is worth $1,000 on a $100,000 portfolio.

Start with $100,000. A +10% quarter takes it to $110,000. A −10% quarter doesn't take $110,000 back down by $10,000 — 10% of the original $100,000 — it takes it down by $11,000, 10% of the new $110,000, landing at $99,000. The percentages are opposite and equal; the dollar amounts they apply to aren't, because the second one starts from a bigger base than the first one did.

This is why compounding uses growth factors, not the percentages themselves. A +10% quarter is a growth factor of 1.10; a −10% quarter is a growth factor of 0.90. Multiply them — 1.10 × 0.90 = 0,99 — and substract 1: −1.00%. That's geometric linking and it's exactly what the formula engine's linkReturns() function does with an array of sub-period returns: multiply every (1+ return) together, then subtract 1 at the end.

It's not a two-period quirk, either. Add a third quarter — the portfolio recovers 5%, taking $99,000 to $103,950. Add up all three percentages (+10%, −10%, +5%) and naive addition says +5.00%. Link them geometrically instead — 1.10 × 0.90 × 1.05, minus 1 — and the real answer is +3,95%. Every additional period widens the gap between the two methods a little more.

Here's the check: this whole example uses nothing but clean, cash-flow-free quarters, so simpleReturn() applied directly to the very first and very last value should agree with the linked answer. $100,000 to $103,950, a straight through: simpleReturn(100000, 103950) = 3,95%. It does agree, exactly. Geometric linking isn't a different method from simple return — it's how you reach the same right answer when you only have interim checkpoints instead of one clean start and end.

  • Period returns compound; they don't add. Linking two returns means multiplying their growth factors — (1 + r1) × (1 + r2) — then subctracting 1, not summing the percentages themselves.
  • A gain and an equal, opposite loss don't cancel out: +10% then −10% nets to −1.00%, because the loss applies to a bigger base than the gain did.
  • The formula engine's linkReturns() takes an array of period returns and compounds them. It doesn't know or care why the periods where split — only how to combine returns once you already have them.
  • Linking clean, cash-flow-free periods always reproduces the same answer as simpleReturn() applied directly to the first and last value — geometric linking is how you reach that answer from interim checkpoints instead of two clean endpoints.
  • The gap between naive addition and geometric linking grows with more periods and with larger swings — it's never just a rounding difference.
