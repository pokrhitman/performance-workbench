Performance for Fund Units (Non-ETF)

Everything so far in this tier has assumed you can see the portfolio itself — its total value, its cash flows. Most retail fund investors never see that. What they get is a published price: a unit value, a NAV per share, updated daily or weekly, with no visibility into the fund's own holdings or transactions. unitValueReturn(), already sitting in the formula engine since the Introduction tier, chains those published prices the same way Time-Weighted Return chains sub-periods — except it turns out there's barely anything to chain. With no cash flow to strip out and no interior valuation that changes the arithmetic, linking simple returns between consecutive prices telescopes exactly back to a single comparison of the first price and last: (v₁/v₀) × (v₂/v₁) × (v₃/v₂) = v₃/v₀ in between cancels out. For a fund that never pays a distribution, there was never a real chaining problem to solve.

There is exactly one thing that breaks that cancellation: a distribution. When a fund pays out income or capital gains, its unit value drops roughly the payout amount — that money left the fund and, unlike a portfolio's own cash flow, there' no separate ledger entry to strip out before comparing prices. The published convention worldwide is to assume the distribution was immediately invested, in full, at that date's post-distribution unit value, buying a few more units rather than handing the investor cash to spend. Reinvestment itself is not a source of return — buying units at the current price with the current price's own cash payout doesn't create value — but it is the only thing standing between a reader and a genuinely misleading number if they eyeball the raw price chart instead.

Run a real example: a fund's own reported NAV falls from 100.00 to 94.00 to 92.00, and then rises to 97.00 — a plain price chart says this fund lost 3.00% over the nine months. But on 1 July, at that 92.00 valuation, the fund also paid a 5.00-per-unit distribution. Reinvested immediately at 92.00, that buys roughly 0.0543 additional units for every unit alread held, so the position that started worth 100.00 ends worth 102.27, no 97.00 — a total return of +2.27%, not −3.00%. Same fund, same nine months, same published prices. The only difference is whether the distribution was reinvested into the count of units the reader is implicitly assuming they still hold one of.

This reinvested-total-return technique is not a German invention, a US invention, or anyone's invention in particular — it is simply how retail fund performance gets reported almost everywhere published prices, not portfolio ledgers, are what an investor actually sees. What genuinely differs by jurisdiction is a question this project keeps returning to: which costs are already embedded in that published number, and which still have to be applied on top. Three named conventions make three different calls on exactly that question. BVI, published by Germany's Bundesverband Investment und Asset Management, is an industry convention, not law: every fund-level cost (management fees, custody, trading) is already baked into the published unit value it chains, and it explicitly stops there — the investor's own Ausgabeaufschlag (front-load) is left out, because it varies by distribution channel and would make funds incomparable if it were baked in. BVI's own published example makes the mechanism concrete: 50 units, a €3-per-unit distribution, reinvested at a post-distribution unit value of €102, buys 150 / 102 ≈ 1.471 new units — a 2.9% increase in units held,exactly the same arithmetic run above.

The SEC's standardized return (US, Form N-1A, Item 26(b) (1)) is the opposite kind of thing — a binding disclosure rule, not an industry norm — and it makes the opposite cost choice on the one question that actually varies: a fund's required "average annual total return" is solved from a hypothetical $1,000 payment run through P(1+T)n = ERV, where that $1,000 is what the investor actually pays, maximum sales load already deducted. The headline number a US fund is required to quote already has the worst-case front-load built in; a “without sales load” figure is shown only as a secondary disclosure alongside it. BVI's German fund and an SEC fund can report the exact same underlying NAV performance and still publish two headline numbers that disagree, not because either is wrong, but because one includes the entry cost and the other deliberately excludes it.

A third convention matters directly to a German-market audience: plenty of the funds actually sold in Germany are not German at all, but domiciled in Luxembourg or Ireland under the EU's UCITS framework, disclosed through a KID (Key Information Document, under the EU's PRIIPs regulation). On the specific question this page keeps asking — which costs are already in the number — a UCITS fund's past performance is shown net of its ongoing charges (already deducted, same embedding choice as BVI) and excludes entry and exit costs from that calculation (same exclusion as BVI). BVI and the EU's UCITS disclosure regime make the same cost-layering choice through two entirely different mechanisms — one an industry convention, the other a binding EU-wide regulation revised more than once since 2018, most recently in 2023. This page doesn't try to track every detail of exactly how that history is presented today; the point that matters here is the cost choice, and on that question KID and BVI agree while SEC's standardized return deliberately doesn't.

One more wrinkle is worth seeing computed, not just described, because it's a genuine trap: a front-load can be quoted as a percentage of two different bases, and the same nominal rate produces two different investor-level numbers. BVI's own published adjustment expresses the load as a percentage of NAV — a 5% load on a 100 NAV means a 105 offering price, so the adjustment divides the fund's cumulative result by (1 + load). The SEC's ERV formula expresses the same kind of fee as a percentage of the offering price itself — a 5% load there means only 95% of what the investor paid was actually invested at NAV. Feed the same 5% and the same underlying return through both conventions on the Interactive tab and watch the investor-level number move by a meaningful amount depending only on which base was meant.

One clarification, since this project's other pages live in the institutional world of GIPS composites: everything above is about publicly reported fund performance — the number printed in a factsheet or a KID, built from published unit price anyone can see. It is a genuinely different problem from the institutional Time-Weighted Return this tier opened with, built from a portfolio's own internal valuations that only the manager (and now, GIPS) can see directly. Both are legitimate; they answer to different audiences.

BVI and EU KID/UCITS show the identical headline figure here on purpose — not a coincidence, but the whole point: they make the same cost-layering choice by two different routes.

  • unitValueReturn(entries) chains raw published prices with no reinvestment step — correct for an accumulating share class (one that never distributes), but it will understate a distributing fund's real performance, since it has nothing to chain besides the endpoints.
  • unitValueTotalReturn(entries) is the distribution-aware version behind BVI, SEC-standardized and EU KID/UCITS returns alike: every distribution is assumed reinvested in full at that date's post-distribution unit value.
  • applyFrontLoad(cumulativeReturn, loadRate, basis) converts a fund-level return into an investor-level one. The "nav" basis (BVI's own convention) and the "offeringPrice" basis (the SEC's convention) express the identical nominal load rate against two different amounts, and are not interchangeable.
  • With zero distributions in the data, unitValueTotalReturn() reduces exactly to simpleReturn() on the first and last unit values — the same identity Modified Dietz and Time-Weighted Return also satisfy when nothing moves during the period.
  • This page covers publicly reported fund-unit performance, not institutional GIPS composites — the shared verification widget's Time-Weighted Return and Modified Dietz pages cover that side, built from a portfolio's own internal valuations rather than a published price series.
  • The NAV-vs-market-price wrinkle specific to exchange-traded funds — where a fund can have two simultaneously “correct” prices — is deliberately left to the next chapter, Performance for Fund Units (ETFs).
