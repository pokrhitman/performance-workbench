Cash-Flow Timing and Sub-Period Breaks

Time-Weighted Return gave a clear instruction: split the period at every cash flow, using the value right before that flow, then chain the clean sub-periods together. What that instruction quietly assumes is that you have a real valuation dated exactly on the cash flow's real date. This chapter is about what happens when you don't.

Take a portfolio worth a $100,000 on January 1, growing at a steady underlying rate that would compound to exactly +8.00% over the next 60 days if nothing else happened. On February 15 — day 45 of 60 — a $20,000 contribution lands. Split the period exactly there, on the real date: the portfolio grows on its own to $105,941.91, the $20,000 arrives to bring it to $125,941.91 and it grows on from there to $128,388.53 by March 2. Two clean sub-periods — +5.94% then 1.94% — chain back to exactly 8.00%. That's TWR working exactly as designed, because the split fell on an actual valuation.

Now suppose the reporting system doesn't hold a valuation for every date a transfer happens to clear — only a fixed month-end snapshot, January 31. The $20,000 gets recorded against that date instead, because that's the only interim value on file, even though the money actually arrived two weeks later. Split there instead: the real January 31 balance — $103,923.05, which genuinely doesn't include the deposit yet, since it hadn't arrived — has $20,000 subtracted from it anyway, producing a first sub-period of −16.08%. A loss that never happened. The second sub-period, now carrying both the deposit's arrival and its own two weeks of growth that actually belonged to the first sub-period, shows +23.54%. Chain those two invented numbers together and the reported return is +3.68% — less than half the true +8.00%.

Nothing about the market or the manager changed between these two versions. Only the recorded date of one cash flow moved by two weeks. Because a sub-period's return is only ever as correct as the valuation defining its edge, attaching a real dollar amount to the wrong date doesn't spread the resulting error evenly across the period — it concentrated the whole thing into the two sub-periods that now border a split that never actually happened. See it live, and watch exactly how the size of the distortion depends on where the true date falls, on the Interactive tab.

Two different fixed are coming. Modified Dietz doesn't need a valuation dated exactly on the cash flow at all — it works from only a start value, an end value, and each flow's real rate, weighting every flow by the fraction of the period it was outstanding. Money-Weighted Return goes further still: no interim valuation whatsoever,just dated cash flows and a final value. Both exist because "use the nearest available snapshot" — the shortcut that just cost four and a third points above — isn't actually safe.

The Correct TWR never moves, no matter where you drag the slider — that's what a genuinely clean split guarantees. The Naive TWR only matches it when the real date happens to land on January 31, and drifts further the farther away it gets — with a sudden jump the moment the real date crosses that boundary, since the entire $20,000 gets misattributed at once rather than gradually.

  • A TWR sub-period split must fall exactly on the cash flow's real date — using the nearest available valuation instead of one taken on that date produces a different, no-less-confident-looking-number.
  • Misattributing a cash flow's date doesn't spread its distortion evenly across the period — it concentrates entirely into the two sub-periods that now border a split that never actually happened.
  • The size of the distortion isn't proportional to how far off the recorded date is: crossing a fixed reporting boundary reassigns the entire cash-flow amount to the wrong side at once, which can move the reported return more than drifting further away without crossing it.
  • This exact mismatch — a batch or reproting date versus the real transfer date — is a realistic root cause behind "your performance number doesn't match ours" disputes, not just a theoretical edge case.
  • Modified Dietz and Money-Weighted Return, next in this tier, exist specifically because neither one needs a valuation dated precisely on the cash-flow date the way TWR does.
