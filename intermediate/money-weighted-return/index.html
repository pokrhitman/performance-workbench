Money-Weighted Return

Time-Weighted Return answers one question: how would $1, invested from day one and left alone, have performed — with every trace of when the client added or removed money stripped out. Money-Weighted Return answers a different question entirely: given exactly when and how much the investor actually put in and took out, what rate did their real dollars earn? Neither answer is more correct. They're not even measuring the same thing.

Take the same portfolio from the Time-Weighted Return chapter: $100,000 on January 1, down to $90,000 by April 1, a $50,000 contribution brings it to $149,000 on July 1, then down again to $134,100 by October 1. TWR chained the two clean −10% legs to −10.90% over the roughly nine-month period. Money-Weighted Return asks something else: from the investor's own wallet, $100,000 left on January 1 and another $50,000 left on July 1 — two real outflows — and $134,100 came back on October 1, the one real inflow. Solve for the single annual rate that discounts those three dated amounts back to exactly zero and the answer is −17.64%

Part of that gap is just units. Money-Weighted Return, by how it's built, is always an annual rate — every cash flow gets discounted on a 365-day-year-basis, regardless of how long the actual period was. TWR's −10.90% above is the raw return over the real nine-month period, not annualized. Put TWR on the same annual footing (the same annualize() from the Beginner tier, applied to the same nine months) and it comes to −14.30% — closer to Money-Weighted Return's −17.64%, but still a real 3-point gap. That remaining gap isn't an artifact of the math. It's because the client's $50,000 landed right before the second −10% leg, so a disproportionate share of their actual money sat through that specific decline. Their real dollar- weighted experience genuinely was worse than the manager's two clean legs, averaged, would suggest.

Notice what didn't need to happen here: no interim valuation requirement. Money-Weighted Return doesn't care whether anyone has a portfolio value dated exactly on July 1 the way TWR does — it only needs the dates and amounts of the investor's actual cash flows, plus a final value. That's the fix the last chapter was building toward.

There's no closed-form formula that solves for that rate directly, the way simpleReturn() or linkReturns() do. irrReturn() finds it numerically instead — starting from a guess, refining it step by step (Newton-Raphson) until the error is smaller than a hair's width. See exactly which dated amounts it's solving against and watch both returns update side by side for this data or your own, on the Interactive tab.

  • Money-Weighted Return (via irrReturn()) solves for the single annualized rate that makes an investor's own dated cash flows discount to exactly zero — a fundamentally different question from TWR's "how would $1 invested from day one have performed".
  • Unlike TWR, Money-Weighted Return is supposed to move when contribution or withdrawal timing and size change — it's capturing the investor's actual dollar-weighted experience, not isolating the manager's skill from it.
  • IRR only cares about dates where money actually moved, plus the final value — an interim valuation with no real cash flow contributes nothing to the calculation, unlike TWR, where every valuation point defines a sub-period boundary.
  • IRR has no closed-form solution — perf-calculations.js finds it numerically (Newton-Raphson) and a genuinely pathological cash-flow pattern can occasionally fail to converge.
  • Neither TWR nor MWR is "more correct" — they answer different questions for different audiences, the same distinction Why Numbers Disagree opened with.
