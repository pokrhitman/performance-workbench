Performance for Fund Units (ETFs)

The previous chapter rested on one quiet assumption: a fund has exactly one price. For a traditional fund that is true — you subscribe and redeem with the fund itself, at the unit value it publishes. An exchange-traded fund breaks that assumption. It still computes a NAV once a day from its holdings, exactly like any other fund. But almost nobody buys or sells at that NAV. Investors trade ETF shares with each other on an exchange, through a broker, at a market price set by supply and demand, all day long. One fund, two prices, and nothing forces them to be equal at any given moment.

What keeps them close is an arbitrage built into the structure. A small group of large dealers — authorized participants — can deal with the fund directly, in big blocks called creation units, at NAV, exchanging baskets of the underlying securities for newly created ETF shares, or the reverse. If the market price rises above NAV, an authorized participant can create shares at NAV and sell them on the exchange at the higher price, which pushes the price back down; below NAV, it buys cheap shares on the exchange and redeems them with the fund. The leftover gap is the premium (market above NAV) or discount (market below NAV), quoted as a percentage of NAV: market price ÷ NAV − 1. For a liquid equity ETF it is usually a small fraction of a percent.

“Usually” is doing real work in that sentence. On 12 March 2020, in the COVID-19 sell-off, the iShares iBoxx $ Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (LQD) closed at a 5.0% discount to its NAV — a fund that, according to its own issuer's data, never closed more than 1% away from NAV in either direction through 2018 and 2019. By the next day the gap had shrunk back to about 0.2%. Which price was “right” that day is still argued over: one side reads the discount as panic selling, the other as the ETF pricing the bond market faster than a NAV built from stale, rarely-traded bond quotes could. That argument is exactly why this chapter exists. It is not a case of one price being a mistake; both are correct answers to different questions.

Because there are two prices, an ETF publishes two returns. A NAV total return chains the NAV series with distributions reinvested at NAV — exactly the unitValueTotalReturn() mechanism from the previous chapter. A market-price total return runs the identical calculation on the market-price series, with distributions reinvested at the market price. iShares' own US fund reports state the convention in exactly those terms, and note that the market return does not include brokerage commissions. The two numbers are not competing methods. They use one method on two different price series.

Run a real example. An ETF's own NAV moves 50.00 → 50.60 → 49.40 → 51.00 over nine months, paying a 0.75-per-share distribution on the 49.40 date. Over the same dates its market price moves 50.10 → 50.65 → 49.30 → 49.98: a 0.20% premium on day one, a 2.00% discount on the last. Reinvesting the distribution in each series gives a NAV total return of +3.55% and a market-price total return of +1.28% — a 2.27-percentage-point gap from a fund whose holdings did exactly the same thing in both calculations. Almost all of the gap is the premium at the start and the discount at the end: 1.0355 × (1 − 0.0200) ÷ (1 + 0.0020 ) − 1 ≈ +1.28%. With no distribution in between, that identity would be exact; here the distribution was reinvested at a market price slightly under NAV, which explains the tiny remainder. The premium path in the middle of the period barely matters. The two ends are what count.

The cost-layering question this project keeps asking has a clean ETF version. The fund's running costs (its total expense ratio) come out of the fund's assets, so they are already embedded in the NAV and therefore in both published returns alike — no difference from any other fund. What the NAV return leaves out, and the market-price return puts in, is the premium or discount. What both published returns leave out is the cost of trading itself. Bought on an exchange, an ETF share carries no front-load at all: the investor isn't subscribing with the fund, so there is no issue price for a load to sit on. The bid-ask spread takes its place in the cost stack. Published market-price returns use a single closing or midpoint price, but a real buyer pays the ask, above that price, and a real seller receives the bid, below it. Put a 0.20% round-trip spread on the example above and the investor's own result is +1.08%, not +1.28%. Brokerage commissions come on top of that and depend on the broker, not the fund, so this page leaves them out.

Regulators handle the two-price problem through disclosure, and the US and EU approach it from different angles. In the US, SEC Rule 6c-11 (adopted 2019) requires most ETFs to publish on their website, every business day, the prior day's NAV, market price and premium or discount; a table and line chart of how often the fund traded at a premium or discount over the last calendar year and the current year's quarters; and the median bid-ask spread over the last 30 calendar days. If the premium or discount stays above 2% for more than seven consecutive trading days, the fund must say so and discuss what it believes caused it. In the EU, ESMA's guidelines on ETFs and other UCITS issues require a UCITS ETF to carry the “UCITS ETF” identifier, to say where its indicative NAV (an intraday estimate of NAV) is published and how it is calculated, and — if the exchange price significantly varies from NAV — to let investors who bought on the exchange sell directly back to the fund, at NAV less costs that must not be excessive. The US rule is built around showing the gap. The EU guidelines are built around giving the investor a way out of it.

So which number is “correct”? All three are, for different questions. The NAV return measures the fund: how well the portfolio tracked its index after its own costs. The market-price return measures the share: what a holder would have earned trading at the published closing prices. The investor return measures one specific trade, spread included. The recurring dispute — “my ETF did worse than the factsheet says” — is very often a client comparing the third number with the first, when the difference is a premium at purchase, a discount at sale and a spread paid twice. Nothing was miscalculated.

The investor column assumes one purchase at the ask on the first date and one sale at the bid on the last, with the distribution reinvested at the market price — the same assumption the published market-price return makes.

  • An ETF has two prices for the same share: a NAV, computed once a day from its holdings, and a market price, set by trading on the exchange. Investors normally trade at the market price.
  • premiumDiscount(nav, marketPrice) returns market price ÷ NAV − 1 — positive for a premium, negative for a discount. Authorized participants' ability to create and redeem at NAV is what normally keeps it small.
  • The NAV total return and the market-price total return are both unitValueTotalReturn(), run on two different price series, with distributions reinvested at NAV and at market price respectively. There is no separate formula for either.
  • With no distributions, the two returns differ by exactly the change in premium/discount: (1 + market return) = (1 + NAV return) × (1 + ending premium) ÷ (1 + starting premium). Only the two ends matter, not the path.
  • applyRoundTripSpread(cumulativeReturn, spreadRate) applies one purchase at the ask and one sale at the bid: (1 + return) × (1 − spread÷2) ÷ (1 + spread÷2) − 1. It plays the role for an ETF that applyFrontLoad() plays for a traditional fund.
  • US: SEC Rule 6c-11 requires daily website disclosure of NAV, market price and premium/discount, a premium/discount history, the 30-day median bid-ask spread, and an explanation if the premium/discount exceeds 2% for more than seven consecutive trading days. EU: ESMA's ETF guidelines require disclosure of where the indicative NAV is published and a direct-redemption route at NAV if the exchange price significantly varies from it.
